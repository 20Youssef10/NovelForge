# Mystery Fairness Reference

Detail for `mystery-engine`. Load when testing whether a reveal is earned, or when a mystery has stalled and needs diagnosis. The skill body carries the four separations and the lifecycle; this file carries the fairness method and failure taxonomy.

## The solvability test

Assemble the evidence available to the reader **at the point of the reveal**, and ask whether a careful reader could have reached the intended conclusion.

1. List every clue presented before the reveal, in order of appearance.
2. Mark each as explicit, observable, or merely inferable.
3. Attempt the intended conclusion using only that set.
4. Note where a different conclusion remains equally available.

If step 3 fails and no in-world reason exists for the evidence being unavailable, the reveal is unearned. The remedy is to plant earlier, not to explain later.

## Deliberate unsolvability

A mystery may be intended to resist solution. That is legitimate when the text establishes **why** the evidence was hidden, destroyed, mislaid, or unreliable — and establishes it visibly, not merely in the author's head. The exception must be legible to the reader at the time it applies.

## Information classes

| Class | Meaning | Risk if mishandled |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explicit | Stated directly | None, but wasted if it removes mystery |
| Observable | Presented as evidence, unexplained | Under-signalled and read as noise |
| Inferable | Supportable by reasoning | The correct home for fair clues |
| Ambiguous | Supports more than one reading | The usual site of fairness problems |
| Hidden | Deliberately withheld | Accidental withholding |
| Misleading | Supports a false conclusion | Unfair if the text never marks the misdirection |

The boundary between **inferable** and **ambiguous** is where most mystery failures live. If a reasonable reader can only reach the intended conclusion by assuming the author is steering them, the reveal is not earned.

## Failure taxonomy

| Failure | What it looks like | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accidental withholding | Evidence exists but the text hides it for effect | Plant it earlier, or establish in-world why it is unavailable |
| Impossible knowledge | A character deduces what the text never gave them | Route the information through a real acquisition, or remove the deduction |
| Arbitrary reveal | A solution arrives with no mechanism | Supply the mechanism, or make the absence of one the point |
| Retroactive meaning | Clues acquire meaning only after the fact, presented as planted | Re-plant visibly, or accept the reveal as a re-reading rather than a payoff |
| Contradictory clues | Two clues cannot both support the solution | Reconcile or remove one; record the choice |
| Orphaned red herring | A false lead is dropped without resolution | Resolve or explain its abandonment |
| Reveal without consequence | The reveal changes nothing for anyone | Track post-reveal knowledge states and their consequences |
| Double-blindness | The reader and all characters are equally in the dark with no accumulating evidence | Add observable evidence; ambiguity without accumulation is not mystery, it is withholding |

## Layered clues

A layered clue reads innocently in context while establishing something deeper. Two requirements:

1. The **surface reading must remain genuinely available** to the reader.
2. The reader must not be told which reading to prefer.

Violating the second turns a clue into a puzzle. Violating the first turns it into a lie.

## Red herrings

A red herring is a real false lead, not a lie. It should be:

- supported by evidence a reasonable reader would follow,
- consistent with the world,
- **resolvable** — either discarded or explained, never merely abandoned.

## Multi-solution mysteries

When more than one conclusion is intended to survive, record which readings are live at each stage and where the text disambiguates. A mystery that resolves by authorial fiat after advertising ambiguity is a broken contract with the reader.
